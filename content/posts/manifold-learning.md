In the previous post we constructed random walks and Brownian motion on a Riemannian manifold by exploiting a given metric \(g\). The exponential map, the Laplace–Beltrami operator, and the heat kernel all flowed from the known geometry. But what if the manifold itself is unknown? Suppose we are only given a point cloud
\[
\{x_1,\dots,x_N\} \subseteq \mathbb{R}^D,
\]
and we suspect that the data lie on (or near) a smooth manifold \(\mathcal{M}\) of dimension \(d \ll D\). This is the central premise of **manifold learning**, also called non-linear dimension reduction. Instead of a prescribed Riemannian metric, we must *infer* the geometry: geodesic distances, the Laplace–Beltrami operator, the intrinsic coordinatesdirectly from the samples. The resulting algorithms produce a faithful low-dimensional representation while respecting the non-linear structure of the data.

---

### The manifold hypothesis and the need for an intrinsic metric

Many high-dimensional observations are generated by a small number of latent variables. A face image with thousands of pixels may change only through lighting and pose; a spoken phoneme sweeps through a low-dimensional articulatory space. Formally, we adopt the following working assumption.

{{< def >}}
**Manifold hypothesis.** The data points \(x_i\) are sampled independently from a probability measure supported on a compact \(d\)-dimensional smooth Riemannian manifold \((\mathcal{M},g)\) isometrically embedded in \(\mathbb{R}^D\).
{{< /def >}}

The Euclidean metric of the ambient space \(\mathbb{R}^D\) does not, in general, reflect the intrinsic distance along \(\mathcal{M}\). Two points that appear far apart in \(\mathbb{R}^D\) may be close when one is forced to walk along the manifold, the classic “Swiss roll” is a prototypical example. The true geometric distance is the geodesic distance
\[
d_\mathcal{M}(x,y) = \inf\Big\{ \int_0^1 \sqrt{g_{\gamma(t)}(\dot\gamma(t),\dot\gamma(t))}\,dt \;:\; \gamma(0)=x,\;\gamma(1)=y \Big\},
\]
exactly the quantity we relied upon when defining the geodesic random walk. Without an explicit manifold, we cannot compute geodesics analytically. The key insight of manifold learning is that local Euclidean distances between neighbouring points approximate the true metric: a sufficiently small neighbourhood of a smooth manifold looks almost like a piece of its tangent space. By gluing together these local approximations, we can recover the global geometry.

### Isomap: from local neighbourhoods to global geodesics

Isomap (Tenenbaum, de Silva & Langford, 2000) turns the above intuition into an algorithm by building a graph that mimics the manifold’s metric structure, then extracting coordinates that best preserve the estimated geodesic distances.

{{< def >}}
**Isomap algorithm.**
1. Connect each point to its \(k\) nearest neighbours (or all points within radius \(\varepsilon\)) to form a graph \(G\); the weight of an edge is the Euclidean distance \(\|x_i-x_j\|\).
2. For every pair of points, estimate the geodesic distance \(d_{ij}\) as the length of the shortest path in \(G\).
3. Apply classical multidimensional scaling (MDS) to the matrix of squared estimated geodesic distances, yielding a low-dimensional embedding \(\{y_i\}\subset \mathbb{R}^d\) that preserves the \(d_{ij}\) as faithfully as possible.
{{< /def >}}

The justification for this procedure is a convergence theorem that guarantees the graph shortest-path distances approximate the true geodesic distances as the sampling density increases.

{{< theorem >}}
**Convergence of graph distances (Bernstein et al., 2000).** Let \(\mathcal{M}\) be a compact smooth submanifold of \(\mathbb{R}^D\) and suppose the data are sampled from a density that is bounded away from zero and infinity on \(\mathcal{M}\). For a suitably chosen neighbourhood radius \(\varepsilon \sim N^{-1/(d+2)}\), the ratio
\[
\frac{d_G(x_i,x_j)}{d_\mathcal{M}(x_i,x_j)}
\]
converges to \(1\) in probability, uniformly over all pairs with \(d_\mathcal{M}(x_i,x_j) \ge \delta > 0\), as \(N \to \infty\).
{{< /theorem >}}

Isomap therefore perfectly mirrors the geometric construction of the first post: the graph serves as a discrete proxy for the exponential map, and the MDS embedding recovers, up to rigid motion, the coordinates one would obtain if the true Riemannian metric were known.

### Locally Linear Embedding: preserving tangent space structure

While Isomap targets global geodesic distances, Locally Linear Embedding (LLE; Roweis & Saul, 2000) preserves the local linear relationships that encode the tangent space of the manifold. Each point is assumed to lie in an approximately linear patch spanned by its neighbours, and the same local reconstruction weights should carry over to the low-dimensional embedding.

{{< def >}}
**Locally Linear Embedding (LLE).**
1. For each point \(x_i\), identify its \(k\) nearest neighbours.
2. Find weights \(W_{ij}\) that minimise \(\|x_i - \sum_{j \in N(i)} W_{ij} x_j\|^2\) subject to \(\sum_j W_{ij}=1\).
3. Fix the weights and compute low-dimensional coordinates \(y_i \in \mathbb{R}^d\) by minimising \(\sum_i \|y_i - \sum_j W_{ij} y_j\|^2\).
{{< /def >}}

The weight matrix \(W\) is a discrete encoding of the local geometry. In fact, if the points are sampled uniformly from a Riemannian manifold, the matrix \(I-W\) approximates the Laplace–Beltrami operator \(\Delta\) up to a scaling factor. The low-dimensional coordinates are therefore the eigenfunctions of this discrete Laplacian, a fact that is made explicit in the next algorithm.

### Laplacian Eigenmaps and the heat kernel

Laplacian Eigenmaps (Belkin & Niyogi, 2003) directly construct a graph Laplacian whose eigenvectors provide a smooth parametrisation of the data manifold. The construction intimately mirrors the heat kernel and Brownian motion on a Riemannian manifold.

{{< def >}}
**Laplacian Eigenmaps.**
1. Build a nearest-neighbour (or \(\varepsilon\)-ball) graph. For each edge, set the weight using the heat kernel
   \[
   w_{ij} = \exp\Bigl(-\frac{\|x_i-x_j\|^2}{t}\Bigr).
   \]
2. Form the diagonal degree matrix \(D_{ii} = \sum_j w_{ij}\) and the normalised graph Laplacian
   \[
   L_{\text{rw}} = I - D^{-1}W.
   \]
3. Compute the \(d\) eigenvectors \(v_1,\dots,v_d\) corresponding to the smallest non-zero eigenvalues of \(L_{\text{rw}}\); the embedding is given by \(y_i = (v_1(i),\dots,v_d(i))\).
{{< /def >}}

The Gaussian weights are not merely a smooth kernel; they are a direct approximation of the intrinsic heat kernel of the manifold. As we saw in the previous post, the transition density of Brownian motion on \((\mathcal{M},g)\) is the fundamental solution \(p(t,x,y)\) of \(\partial_t u = \frac12 \Delta u\). For small \(t\), the celebrated asymptotic expansion
\[
p(t,x,y) \sim \frac{1}{(2\pi t)^{d/2}} e^{-\frac{d_\mathcal{M}(x,y)^2}{2t}} \bigl(1+O(t)\bigr)
\]
shows that the heat kernel is locally Gaussian in the geodesic distance. Because \(d_\mathcal{M}(x,y) \approx \|x-y\|\) for neighbouring points, the graph weights faithfully capture the short-time transition probabilities of manifold Brownian motion. The graph Laplacian built from these weights converges to the true Laplace–Beltrami operator.

{{< theorem >}}
**Convergence of the graph Laplacian (Belkin & Niyogi, 2007; Hein, Audibert & von Luxburg, 2007).** Under the manifold hypothesis and mild regularity conditions, the normalised graph Laplacian \(L_{\text{rw}}\) converges pointwise and spectrally to the Laplace–Beltrami operator \(\Delta\) on \(\mathcal{M}\) as \(N\to\infty\) and the neighbourhood radius \(\varepsilon \to 0\).
{{< /theorem >}}

Consequently, the eigenvectors produced by Laplacian Eigenmaps are estimates of the eigenfunctions of \(\Delta\), the same operator that governs the Brownian motion constructed in the earlier post. These eigenfunctions form a natural “Fourier” basis on the manifold, optimally smooth with respect to the intrinsic geometry.

### t-SNE: matching diffusion probabilities

t-distributed Stochastic Neighbor Embedding (t-SNE; van der Maaten & Hinton, 2008) takes a probabilistic perspective, matching neighbourhood distributions in the high and low-dimensional spaces. Although not a direct spectral method, it is firmly rooted in the same geometric intuitions.

{{< def >}}
**t-SNE algorithm (simplified).**
1. In the high-dimensional space define conditional similarities
   \[
   p_{j|i} = \frac{\exp(-\|x_i-x_j\|^2 / 2\sigma_i^2)}{\sum_{k\neq i} \exp(-\|x_i-x_k\|^2 / 2\sigma_i^2)},
   \qquad
   p_{ij} = \frac{p_{j|i}+p_{i|j}}{2N}.
   \]
   The bandwidths \(\sigma_i\) are chosen adaptively to achieve a user-specified perplexity.
2. In the low-dimensional embedding define pairwise similarities using a heavy-tailed Student-\(t\) distribution with one degree of freedom:
   \[
   q_{ij} = \frac{(1+\|y_i-y_j\|^2)^{-1}}{\sum_{k\neq l} (1+\|y_k-y_l\|^2)^{-1}}.
   \]
3. Minimise the Kullback–Leibler divergence \(\mathrm{KL}(P\|Q) = \sum_{i,j} p_{ij} \log\frac{p_{ij}}{q_{ij}}\) by gradient descent.
{{< /def >}}

The Gaussian affinities \(p_{j|i}\) are, once again, a local approximation of the heat kernel, with the perplexity playing the role of the diffusion time scale. The heavy-tailed kernel \(q_{ij}\) in the embedding space counteracts the “crowding problem”, allowing moderate distances to be represented without collapsing far-apart points. In effect, t-SNE attempts to match the diffusion distances that would be obtained by a random walk on the data manifold.

### From data geometry back to stochastic processes

The interplay between manifold learning and the stochastic analysis of the first post is both natural and deep. Once we have inferred the Laplace–Beltrami operator \(\Delta\), either via a graph Laplacian or through a metric reconstruction, we can construct a random walk or a Brownian motion that is *intrinsic* to the data manifold.

Recall the geodesic random walk with step size \(\epsilon\) defined on a Riemannian manifold \((X,g)\):
\[
X_{k+1}^{\epsilon} = \exp_{X_k^{\epsilon}}\bigl( \epsilon \, V_{k+1} \bigr),
\]
where \(V_{k+1}\) is an isotropic random vector in \(T_{X_k^\epsilon}X\). Its generator converged to \(\frac12 \Delta\). If we now replace the true exponential map and metric with their discrete approximations learned from data, we obtain a Markov chain that explores the point cloud in a geometrically faithful way. The transition probabilities are supplied by the graph Laplacian itself, or by a nearest-neighbour graph that respects the learned geodesic distances.

Such data-driven diffusions are much more than a visualisation tool. They enable sampling, clustering, and semi-supervised learning that respect the curved geometry of the data. The heat kernel on the learned manifold provides a natural measure of similarity that outperforms raw Euclidean distance in high-dimensional settings. Moreover, the eigenfunctions of the Laplacian serve as intrinsic coordinates for solving partial differential equations on the data, including the heat equation that appeared in the scaling limit of the random walk.
